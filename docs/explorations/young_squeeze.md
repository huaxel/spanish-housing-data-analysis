# The young-adult squeeze: collapse, refill, and who forms households

Computed by `explorations/young_squeeze.py` (`artifacts/exploration_03.json`).
Ages from ECP single-year detail, bands parsed with overlap guards
(`scripts/parse_edad.py`: 85–99 singles and centenarians live inside the
`85 y más` grouped series — verified arithmetically, tolerance 2e-4).

## Collapse (2007–2019), then a two-speed refill (2019–2025)

The 20–34 cohort fell everywhere 2007–19 (−15% Balears to −41% Asturias;
nationally 10.5M→7.7M) — Spain's baby-bust generations aging out, barely
offset by immigration. The 2019–25 refill split the country: Madrid (+17%),
Cataluña (+16%), Valencia (+14%) rebounded on migration; the interior kept
shrinking (Extremadura −8.7%, Asturias −2.5%, Galicia −1.9%).

Young share of population in 2025 runs 19.1% (Balears) to 12.6% (Asturias) —
and the ranking mirrors affordability pain: the four highest young shares
(Balears, Madrid, Canarias, Murcia/Cataluña) include the three worst
affordability readings. The young flock to expensive magnets for jobs; they
don't arbitrage housing costs away.

## 2021–2025: two different household surges

In Madrid (+160k young / +162k households) and Cataluña (+160k / +171k) the
household surge *is* young migrants. Everywhere else it isn't: Andalucía
added 165k households on only +22k young adults; Galicia +37k on +4k;
Castilla y León +33k on +8k. There, growth comes from shrinking household
size (splits, solo living, longer lives) and older migrants — demand that
new construction (mostly family-sized, where it happens at all) matches badly.

This sharpens Hypothesis 01's verdict: the absorption ratio's denominator
isn't just "demand" in the abstract — in the magnets it's young migrants,
in the interior it's household fragmentation. Same national shortfall,
different housing needs.
